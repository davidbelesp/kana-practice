# Katakana in the world around you

Katakana maps to the same core sounds as hiragana, but its angular shapes signal a different role. It commonly writes words borrowed from other languages, names from outside Japan, sound effects, and emphasized terms.

## Familiar sounds, new shapes

The vowel order and consonant rows remain useful. Dakuten and handakuten work here too: カ → ガ and ハ → パ. Contracted sounds use small ャ・ュ・ョ, as in ジュ (**ju**).

## The long-vowel mark

The bar ー extends the preceding vowel and takes one mora: ケーキ is **kēki**. Do not read the bar as a dash or skip its timing.

## Adapting foreign sounds

Small vowels let katakana approximate combinations outside the traditional chart. Examples include ファ (**fa**), ティ (**ti**), and チェ (**che**). These are Japanese sound patterns adapted for loanwords, not exact copies of every source-language pronunciation.

Pay special attention to visually close pairs such as シ/ツ and ソ/ン. Compare stroke direction and position, then practice recognition in complete words.
