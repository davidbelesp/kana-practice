# A map of written Japanese

Japanese reading becomes easier when you stop expecting one alphabet to do every job. A normal sentence can use several scripts, with each one giving the reader a different clue.

## Four useful lenses

- **Hiragana** represents Japanese sounds and commonly writes grammatical endings, particles, and many native words.
- **Katakana** represents the same basic sound inventory in a different shape. It often marks loanwords, sound effects, and deliberate emphasis. Commonly used for foreign words.
- **Kanji** are characters associated with meaning and one or more readings. They make content words compact and recognizable.
- **Rōmaji** writes Japanese with Latin letters. It is useful as a temporary bridge, but kana should gradually replace it.

In <span class="grammar-example"><span class="grammar-topic">私は</span><span class="grammar-about">パンをたべます</span></span>, 私 is kanji, は・を・たべます use hiragana, and パン uses katakana. The mixture is organized, not decorative.

## A practical reading habit

First identify the script, then read the sound, then connect the whole word to meaning. Avoid converting every kana into rōmaji in your head: direct recognition is faster and preserves Japanese rhythm.

> Script choice can vary for style, audience, or emphasis. Learn the common pattern first, then notice real-world exceptions.
